Specimen catalog: Michigan & Ohio

Six spiders you can actually run into around the Great Lakes — in the garden, under a porch light, or turning over a log.

Both states share almost the same spider fauna. Cold winters and the same mix of woodland, farmland, and lakeshore habitat mean a garden in Ann Arbor and one in Columbus will turn up nearly identical residents. Only one species below is worth any real caution.

sunny garden

Black-and-Yellow Garden Spider

Argiope aurantia

Builds a wheel-shaped web anchored by a bold zigzag stitch down the middle, usually in a sunny patch of tall grass or garden edge. Common across both states from midsummer through the first hard frost.

bridges & docks

Furrow Orb Weaver

Larinioides cornutus

Favors the undersides of bridges, porch eaves, and anything near standing water. Tears down and eats most of its own web every morning, then rebuilds it fresh by dusk.

fences & siding

Bold Jumping Spider

Phidippus audax

Hunts by sight instead of silk, stalking prey across fences and siding before pouncing several times its own body length. The chelicerae flash iridescent green in direct sun.

streambanks

Dark Fishing Spider

Dolomedes tenebrosus

One of the largest spiders in the region, often mistaken for a small crab. Rests head-down on tree trunks near streams and can walk out onto the water's surface to ambush prey.

leaf litter

Wolf Spider

Tigrosa & Hogna spp.

Hunts on foot through leaf litter and lawns rather than waiting in a web. Females carry their egg sac attached to the spinnerets, then carry the spiderlings on their back after hatching.

brush piles

Northern Black Widow

Latrodectus variolus

The Great Lakes region's only medically significant spider, though it's shy and rarely bites unless trapped against skin. Look for a broken row of red spots down the back rather than the solid hourglass of its southern relative.
